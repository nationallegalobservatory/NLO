## 01 | JUDGMENT OF THE MONTH

### Sujit Das & Ors. v. Union of India

*2026 INSC 1195 | Decided: November 5, 2026 | Bench: Justice Ujjal Bhuyan and Justice Atul S. Chandurkar*

#### Background

Three men were accused in an NDPS case registered by the Divisional Preventive Force, Customs Division, Agartala. The prosecution said they were intercepted at about 2:00 pm on September 29, 2025 after a tip-off, and that 60.79 kg of methamphetamine tablets were recovered over two seizures, the second completed at 5:00 pm. Their arrest was recorded at 11:00 am and 11:15 am the next day. They were produced before the Magistrate at 4:45 pm.

The Special Judge refused bail in March 2026 and the Tripura High Court did the same in April, both relying on Section 37 of the NDPS Act. The Article 22(2) question was never argued in the High Court. The Supreme Court raised it on its own on September 21 and called for an affidavit from the Investigating Officer. The Union's answer was that the interval went on statutory formalities and that interception is not arrest.

#### What the Court Held

Justices Bhuyan and Chandurkar rejected that distinction. Article 22(2) speaks of a person "arrested and detained in custody," and the Bench read the expression as conjunctive: the two words mean the same thing. The 24 hours therefore start when a person is detained and their liberty is effectively curtailed, not when an arrest is formally recorded. On that count the accused were arrested at 2:00 pm on September 29, and production at 4:45 pm the following day breached the mandate. The 18-hour gap between the end of the search and the recorded arrest was also unexplained and unreasonable under Section 58 BNSS.

> *“The Constitution does not recognize any detention dehors arrest.”*  
> — Justice Ujjal Bhuyan, (2026 INSC 1195)

The Court disapproved the Orissa High Court's decision in *Aryaman Pattnayak*, the Madras High Court's in *Kannan* and the Kerala High Court's in *Arun K. Thomas*, and called the Kerala "buffer zone" reasoning unconstitutional. Only the journey time to the Magistrate may be excluded from the 24 hours. It directed that the three be produced before the Special Court within seven days and released on bail, and laid down duties for Magistrates: record the actual date and time of detention, check whether the 24 hours were respected, and decline remand if the detention ran longer than was reasonable even within the window. Copies go to every Director General of Police, the Registrars General of the High Courts and the State Judicial Academies.

#### Why This Matters

The ruling moves the Article 22(2) inquiry from the arrest memo to the clock. For years the gap between being stopped and being shown as arrested has been where agencies hold a person without the safeguards the Constitution attaches to custody. The Bench treated that gap as custody and tied it to the concern with custodial abuse that runs from *D.K. Basu* onward.

It also shifts a burden onto the Magistrate. A court receiving an accused must now ask when liberty was actually curtailed, and the answer has to be written down. The decision arose under the NDPS Act, but its reasoning applies to any agency that detains first and records arrest later. The order to circulate it to police chiefs and judicial academies suggests the Court expects changes in practice, not just a new citation. NLO will watch whether remand orders start recording detention time, and how the central agencies revise their procedures.

## 02 | CONSTITUTIONAL WATCH

### Key Developments at the Supreme Court | November 2026

#### 1. Freedom From Bondage Is Not Secured by Rescue Alone: Uniform SOP for Bonded Labourers

*Surendra Manjhi & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors. | 2026 INSC 1185 | Decided: November 2, 2026 | Bench: Justice Vikram Nath and Justice Aparesh Kumar Singh*

The Court directed nationwide implementation of a revised Standard Operating Procedure for identifying, releasing and rehabilitating bonded labourers, observing that rescue by itself does not end bondage. The SOP carries fixed timelines: verification of complaints and release certificates ordinarily within 24 hours, immediate financial assistance within seven days of the release certificate, and an individual rehabilitation plan within 90 days of rescue. The writ petitions stay pending so the Court can monitor compliance. The abolition statute has promised rehabilitation for decades. What the Court has added is a clock.

#### 2. Two-Child Norm: Court Says Penalising Laws Have Lost Relevance, Sends Precedent to Larger Bench

*Mangala Bhimrao Ingle v. Additional Commissioner, Amravati Division & Ors. | 2026 INSC 1193 | Decided: November 4, 2026 | Bench: Justice P.S. Narasimha and Justice Alok Aradhe*

The appeal concerned a Sarpanch disqualified under Section 14(1)(j-1) of the Maharashtra Village Panchayats Act, 1959 for having more than two children. The validity of that provision was not challenged. The Bench still observed that laws penalising a third child have lost their relevance because demographic change has removed the fear of overpopulation that justified them, and referred the precedent upholding such restrictions to a larger Bench for reconsideration. This is a reference, not a ruling. The Court did not strike down any law, and the question of whether the restrictions survive now waits on the larger Bench.

#### 3. A Seven-Judge Reference Pending Since 1998 Is Closed as Infructuous

*Arjun Flour Mills v. State of Odisha Finance Department Secretary & Ors. | 2026 INSC 1192 | Decided: November 4, 2026 | Bench: CJI Surya Kant, Justices S.V.N. Bhatti, Joymalya Bagchi, N.V. Anjaria, Arun Palli, V. Mohana and K.V. Viswanathan*

A seven-judge Bench closed a reference on the validity of surcharges computed on sales tax payable, which had been pending since 1998. The Court held that a reference survives only as long as the doubt that occasioned it. The nine-judge decision in *Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India* (2024) held that royalty is not a tax and overruled *India Cement* to that extent, removing the premise on which the question was referred. Sales tax has since been replaced by VAT and then GST, and Entry 54 of List II was substituted by the 101st Amendment, so the question has no prospective significance for the States concerned. *S. Kodar v. State of Kerala* continues to hold the field. [Last month NLO reported two judges describing delay in Constitution Bench references as an institutional embarrassment](/publications/research/monthly-legal-review-october-2026#3-two-judges-tell-the-cji-constitution-bench-delays-are-an-institutional-embarrassment). This closure can be read as sensible housekeeping. It can also be read as the problem in miniature: a question that took 28 years to leave the docket because it stopped mattering.

#### 4. Jailed for Seven Years After Acquittal: Rs. 30 Lakh Awarded, Court Calls for a Compensation Law

*Devilal Gond v. State of Chhattisgarh | Supreme Court of India | Decided: November 16, 2026 | Bench: Justice Augustine George Masih and Justice Sanjeev Sachdeva*

Devilal Gond was acquitted by the Chhattisgarh High Court, but the acquittal never reached the prison authorities. He walked out on May 19, 2025, after seven years, three months and 21 days of detention with no authority of law. The High Court had awarded him Rs. 2 lakh. The Supreme Court raised it to Rs. 30 lakh, payable within four weeks, with Rs. 5 lakh going to his savings account and the rest to a supervised fixed deposit. The Bench called the detention a failure of the criminal justice administration and an egregious violation of Article 21, and noted that India has no statutory framework for compensating wrongful incarceration. It said such a mechanism is needed. *Sujit Das* is about the first 24 hours of custody. This case is about the last seven years.

## 03 | LEGISLATIVE TRACKER

### No Session Yet. The Winter Agenda Is Taking Shape.

Parliament has not sat since August 13. As of November 17 the Winter Session had not been notified. Reports point to the first week of December, and Opposition leaders say they have received no official communication on dates. The Monsoon Session was never prorogued, so the government can either extend it at short notice or prorogue it and summon a fresh session, which would need a couple of weeks of notice to members.

#### Delimitation-Linked Constitution Amendment: Expected Back

NLO has tracked this since July. The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill failed in April, receiving 298 votes against the 352 needed for a two-thirds majority. Reports now say the government wants to bring it back this session. The amendment would allow the Lok Sabha to grow from 543 seats to a maximum of 850 through delimitation, and is linked to implementing 33% reservation for women in Parliament and State legislatures ahead of the 2029 general election. The ruling alliance has about 320 members in a House of 540, so it needs Opposition votes. Nothing has been listed officially yet.

#### FCRA Amendment Bill and the Simultaneous Polls Bill: Committee Reports Due

The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026 has been with a Joint Parliamentary Committee since August 12, which held a stakeholder hearing on October 12. The committee is due to report by the last day of the first week of the Winter Session. The Bill remains pending. Session previews also expect the Joint Committee examining the simultaneous polls proposal to report during this session. NLO will track both reports once the dates are fixed.

## 04 | POLICY PULSE

### A Data Protection Milestone and a Judicial Quality Check

#### DPDP Rules: Consent Manager Registration Goes Live

*Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology | Effective: November 13, 2026*

Rule 4 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 came into force on November 13, along with Sections 6(9) and 27(1)(d) of the Act, one year after the Rules were notified. It opens the registration route for Consent Managers: entities registered with the Data Protection Board that give a person one point from which to give, manage, review and withdraw consent. Only this part commenced. The substantive obligations on data fiduciaries, covering notice, consent, security, breach reporting, retention, children's data, data principal rights and cross-border transfers, start on May 13, 2027. An ordinary business has no new duty from November 13 and is not required to use a Consent Manager. NLO has not found a published window for registration applications.

#### Supreme Court Moots an Audit of High Court Judgments for Remarks on Women

*In Re: Order dated 09.07.2026 passed by the High Court of Judicature at Patna | Supreme Court of India | Hearing: November 16, 2026 | Bench: CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana*

The Court took *suo motu* cognisance of a Patna High Court order that found no overt act establishing an attempt to commit rape, and issued notice on September 23. At the November 16 hearing the CJI said State Judicial Academies could be asked to survey their High Courts' judgments for observations offensive to women, after which the High Courts could be directed to begin *suo motu* proceedings to expunge them. He said the aim is not to set the judgments aside. The Bench was struck that the Patna order came after the Court had set aside a similar Allahabad High Court order and issued guidelines on handling sexual offence cases sensitively. It sought suggestions on guidelines and gender sensitisation training and gave a week for responses. No direction has been issued yet. If it is, the judiciary will be auditing its own writing.

## 05 | EDITOR'S NOTE

### Who Is Keeping Time

*By [Bhoomija Khanna](/bhoomija), Research Director*

[Last month I wrote about a Court that keeps filling gaps others leave](/publications/research/monthly-legal-review-october-2026#05--editors-note). This month the gaps are measured in hours and years. Count the clocks in this issue.

*Sujit Das* says the 24 hours under Article 22(2) start when liberty is curtailed, not when the paperwork says arrest. The bonded labour SOP sets 24 hours, seven days and 90 days. Devilal Gond spent seven years, three months and 21 days in prison after an acquittal because one order never reached one jail. Three different matters, one shared fact: the state had been keeping the time in its own favour, and the Court is now taking the watch back.

The Court keeps its own clocks too. A seven-judge reference that opened in 1998 closed this month, not because anyone answered it but because the question died on the way. I don't read that as the answer to last month's complaint about Constitution Bench delays. It is closer to a case study. Twenty-eight years is a long time to wait for a question to stop mattering.

Then there are the clocks outside the courtroom. The DPDP Rules run on dates written into them: November 13, 2026 for Consent Managers, May 13, 2027 for everyone else. Parliament has not sat for 97 days, and the Winter Session has no date. A legal system can only be as punctual as the slowest institution in it.

That is the weakness in all of this. A Court can write a number into an order. It cannot make the jail, the police station or the Ministry honour it. Gond's case shows it exactly: a correct order, no delivery. The real test of *Sujit Das* is not what the judgment says about 24 hours. It is whether a Magistrate in Agartala or Aurangabad starts asking, every time, what time the person was actually stopped. I will be looking for that answer in the coming issues.
