01 | Judgment of the Month

Just Rights for Children Alliance & Anr. v. S. Harish & Ors.

2026 INSC 1149  |  Decided: October 15, 2026  |  Bench: Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice K. Vinod Chandran

Background

In its 2024 ruling in this same matter, the Supreme Court had read Section 15 of the POCSO Act and Section 67B of the IT Act to cover viewing, storing and failing to report child sexual abuse material, and it urged the Union, through the Ministry of Women and Child Development, to roll out comprehensive sex education covering consent, exploitation and the legal consequences of child pornography. Two years on, the Bench returned to find out what had actually been done.

The answer, on the record before it, was not much. The Court used this hearing to press the Union on its own prior directions, and to go considerably further than it had in 2024, both on the digital-safety architecture needed to detect and remove such material, and on the question of whether, and how, children's own access to the internet can be restricted in the name of protecting them.

What the Court Held

The Bench held that the right to access and use the internet is a fundamental right under Article 21 read with Article 19(1)(a), and that this right belongs to children irrespective of age. It declined to endorse a blanket restriction on children's access to social media, reasoning that safety and freedom are not opposed values a child must trade off against each other. Justices Pardiwala and Vinod Chandran were explicit that the two protections, access and protection from exploitation, are meant to coexist.

children enjoy the same right irrespective of age

Alongside that holding, the Court laid out a wide operational framework for Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse Material (CSEAM): a national Hash and URL List Database with multi-tier human verification, fixed reporting timelines for intermediaries, dedicated CSEAM grievance officers, and a uniform, easily accessible reporting category across platforms. It asked the Union to consider exemplary fines and disgorgement of paywall revenue from platforms that profit off such material, and clarified that CSEAM, for the purposes of the IT Act and Rules, is not confined to visual depictions. Separately, it directed that digital safety and age-appropriate sex education be taught as a single subject from the primary school stage, and listed sixteen measures for the Union, the States, child rights commissions, schools and intermediaries to implement, covering consent, healthy relationships, safe internet use, reporting channels, teacher training, parental awareness, in-school helplines and victim counselling.

Why This Matters

This is a court checking its own homework. The 2024 judgment asked the Union to build a sex-education programme; this order exists because that request appears to have gone nowhere, and the Bench said so plainly by asking the Union directly what it had done since. That is a different posture than issuing a fresh direction into a vacuum: it is enforcement of a standing instruction the Court gave itself the authority to issue two years ago.

The internet-access holding is also likely to outlast the headlines around it. A right that applies to a four-year-old as much as a forty-year-old reframes every future debate about age-gating, parental consent flows and platform verification, because none of those tools can now be defended simply on the ground that children have no claim to the underlying access in the first place. The sixteen-measure CSEAM framework gives the Union a detailed blueprint; whether it gets built this time, or sits alongside the 2024 directions as another item the Court has to ask about again, is what NLO will be watching for.

02 | Constitutional Watch

Key Developments at the Supreme Court | October 2026

1. Delhi-NCR Rape Cases: Suo Motu Proceedings Move From Audits to “War Rooms”

In Re: Recent Rape Incidents in Delhi-NCR | Supreme Court of India | Hearings through October 2026 | Bench: Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice K. Vinod Chandran

The suo motu case the Court opened on September 28, after a string of rape and sexual assault cases across Delhi-NCR, kept expanding through October. Having first ordered a four-week lighting, CCTV and vulnerability-mapping audit, the Bench returned on October 5 to push further: a 24-hour monitoring cell for vulnerable areas, and an explicit instruction that preventing crime, not merely arresting the accused afterward, has to be the priority. By the second week of October the Court had issued a six-point plan directing Delhi Police to set up district-level “war rooms,” intensify beat patrols around educational institutions, and coordinate across police-station boundaries. The Delhi High Court has separately been asked for a full account of Fast Track Special Courts and Exclusive POCSO Courts, with recent cases to be tried within a year. Amicus curiae Liz Mathew continues to assist the Bench, with the matter still listed for further directions.

2. When Can the Election Commission Skip a Bye-Election? Judgment Reserved on Section 151A

Election Commission of India v. [Bombay High Court Direction, Pune Lok Sabha Constituency] | Supreme Court of India | Judgment reserved: October 6, 2026 | Bench: Justice Vikram Nath, Justice Augustine George Masih and Justice Sandeep Mehta

The Court has reserved judgment on how Section 151A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 should be read. The provision sets a six-month window for the Election Commission to fill a casual vacancy by bye-election, subject to an exception where less than a year of the term remains. The ECI had declined to hold a bye-election for the Pune Lok Sabha seat, vacant since March 2023, citing that exception; the Bombay High Court disagreed and ordered the poll held. The question now before the Bench is whether the one-year exception is tested from the date the seat falls vacant or from the date six months later, when the Commission's obligation would otherwise kick in. Whichever way it is decided, the ruling will fix how every future casual vacancy is handled, and petitioners have separately argued the Commission has applied the provision inconsistently across different vacancies. NLO will track the outcome once pronounced.

3. Two Judges Tell the CJI Constitution Bench Delays Are an “Institutional Embarrassment”

Jaya Thakur v. Union of India | Supreme Court of India | Order: September 23, 2026 | Bench: Justice Dipankar Datta and Justice Satish Chandra Sharma

Hearing a challenge to the 2023 law that replaced the Chief Justice of India with a Union Cabinet Minister on the panel appointing Election Commissioners, Justices Datta and Sharma split on whether the matter needed a five-judge Constitution Bench at all. Justice Datta held the governing principles were already settled and no reference was required; Justice Sharma held the opposite. What they agreed on, in a joint order, was that references to Constitution Benches take years to resolve, calling it a source of “institutional embarrassment,” and noting one earlier reference had taken nine years to travel from a seven-judge to a nine-judge bench, with five months more to decide. Both judges asked the CJI to explore constituting a permanent five-judge Bench for constitutional questions. As of this issue, more than a month later, there is no public indication the CJI has acted on the request; this is an institution asking itself to fix a problem it has been flagging, by its own account, for years.

4. AI-Generated Judgments Surface in Court Filing Again

Deepak Raheja and Another v. Omkara Assets Reconstruction Private Limited and Another | 2026 INSC 1134 | Decided: October 15, 2026 | Bench: Justice Dipankar Datta and Justice Augustine George Masih

In two insolvency appeals under Section 62 of the IBC, the Court described as “undesirable” a compilation of judgments filed on behalf of the appellants that allegedly contained several AI-generated, non-existent decisions. It had proposed exemplary costs over the conduct, but ultimately made no order on costs after being informed the first appellant had died. NLO flagged this exact failure mode as its first Judgment of the Month in July, in a case where fabricated citations made it past both a Tribunal and an Appellate Tribunal before anyone checked. This is the second instance to surface in Supreme Court proceedings since; whatever verification practice the Bar is relying on to catch this before filing does not yet appear to be working consistently.

03 | Legislative Tracker

No Session This Month. Two Threads Carry Over.

Parliament was not in session in October; the Monsoon Session closed on August 13 and the Winter Session had not been formally announced as of this issue. That leaves the two items this column flagged for tracking after August with no floor votes to report, but both saw real movement off the floor.

FCRA (Amendment) Bill: Committee Hears Stakeholders

The Joint Parliamentary Committee examining the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026, referred to it on August 12 after sustained opposition from civil society groups, held a stakeholder hearing on October 12 in New Delhi, taking views from NGOs, professional bodies and legal experts. JPC chairman Sanjay Jaiswal said the committee had heard a range of perspectives and would deliberate on them internally. The committee is expected to submit its report by the first day of the Winter Session; the Bill, which would let a Designated Authority take over and dispose of a deregistered organisation's assets without prior judicial approval, remains pending and has not been withdrawn.

Delimitation Bills: Still Shelved

The Delimitation Bill, defeated in April and not reintroduced during the Monsoon Session, remains listed as pending on Parliament's own bill tracker with no indication of when, or whether, the government intends to bring it back. NLO will continue to track both threads into the Winter Session.

04 | Policy Pulse

One Bill Still Waiting, One Office Opens

Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2026: Three Months On, Still No Revised Draft

Bar Council of India | Consultation closed: July 31, 2026

It has now been nearly three months since the BCI's consultation window on the Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2026 closed, and no revised draft incorporating stakeholder input has been released. The Bill still needs Cabinet approval and passage by both Houses before it has any legal effect. The only visible BCI action in the interim was unrelated to the Bill itself: in August, BCI Chairman Manan Kumar Mishra directed State Bar Councils not to enrol 2026 graduates of NALSAR University amid a dispute over CJI Surya Kant's presence at the institution's convocation, then withdrew the directive after it drew criticism, including from within the Bar Council itself, as manifestly arbitrary. NLO will continue to track the Bill's progress.

eCommittee Inaugurates Digital Court Technology Office

eCommittee, Supreme Court of India | October 16, 2026

The Supreme Court's eCommittee inaugurated the Digital Court Technology Office, a centralised body tasked with planning, implementing and supervising all technology initiatives under the eCourts Project, at a ceremony attended by eCommittee chairperson Justice Vikram Nath, Justice Anjani Kumar Mishra, Department of Justice secretary Neeraj Verma and NIC director general Priyank Bharti. The office formalises what has so far been a dispersed set of digitisation efforts, including the National Judicial Data Grid and ongoing e-filing and video-conferencing rollouts, under a single administrative structure. The eCommittee has framed the move as the next phase after April's National Conference on Judicial Process Re-engineering and Digital Transformation, which set out the broader roadmap this office is meant to execute.

05 | Editor's Note

Same Two Judges, Same Instinct

By Bhoomija Khanna, Research Director

In August I wrote about a Bench that built a river authority because Rajasthan never did. This month the same instinct shows up twice over, and in one case it is literally the same two judges doing it: Justices Pardiwala and Vinod Chandran wrote both the headline judgment, where the Court wrote a sixteen-point child-safety curriculum into existence, and the Delhi-NCR suo motu order, where it is now telling Delhi Police to run war rooms and redraw patrol beats. Different subject matter, same bench, same posture.

What is new this month is a third variation on the theme: the Court turning the instinct on itself. Justices Datta and Sharma could not agree on whether one case needed a five-judge Bench, but they agreed on something larger: that the Court's own machinery for resolving constitutional references is broken, badly enough that they used the word “embarrassment” about their own institution. A nine-year wait for a seven-judge reference to become a nine-judge Bench is not a problem any administrator handed down from outside could fix. It can only be fixed from within, which is exactly why they asked the CJI to consider a permanent Bench rather than ordering one, because this is the one kind of institutional gap the Court cannot build around itself through a writ petition.

And then there is the detail that would be easy to miss in a month this full: an AI-hallucinated citation, for the second time since July, sitting inside a filing that reached the Supreme Court before anyone caught it. I did not expect to be writing about that pattern again so soon. Three months in, it is no longer a single alarming case. It is something closer to a known failure mode that keeps recurring in exactly the places meant to catch it first.

None of these three threads are related in substance. A child's right to the internet, a patrol schedule in Delhi, a nine-year-old backlog, a fake citation; there is no shared doctrine connecting them. What connects them is where the fix came from. In every case this month, it was the judiciary noticing a gap, in the executive, in the Bar, or in itself, and filling it because nobody else had. I said in August that this was worth watching past the news cycle. Three issues in, I think it is worth watching for how long the Court can keep doing it before something outside the Court has to.
